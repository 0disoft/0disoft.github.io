John Lee Dumas's reason for starting a podcast was simple: he lacked shows he wanted to listen to while driving, so he decided to make one himself. Entrepreneurs on Fire, or EOFire for short, grew into a business that generated 120,570 USD in podcast sponsorship revenue in December 2024.

## Making the Show He Wanted to Hear Every Day

Before that, Dumas had held several jobs. He served four years as an active-duty officer in the U.S. Army, entered law school but dropped out in his first semester, and also worked in corporate finance and commercial real estate. While working in real estate, he spent long hours driving to meet clients and see properties, and podcasts filled that time.

He became especially absorbed in interviews in which founders talked about their businesses and lives. The problem was that his favorite programs came out once a week or every other week. Dumas wanted to hear a new story every day, and one day he finished all the episodes he had queued up. The idea came from there: for people like him who wanted to hear founders' experiences while commuting or traveling, he would publish an interview every day.

He quit his job in June 2012 and began preparing the show. Using savings he had accumulated over several years, he covered living expenses and startup costs while spending on mentoring, equipment, a website, and outside help. From June to September 2012, he spent 9,961.73 USD, while revenue in the same period was 0 USD.

## When the Guest's Audience Becomes the Next Listener

A mentor played a major role in finding his first guests. Jaime Masters, whom Dumas hired, suggested he attend the Blog World event in New York, where she introduced him to Pat Flynn, Derek Halpern, and others. Dumas described the show he was building and asked them to appear. Although his show was not yet known online, well-known founders agreed to come on.

EOFire's first episode was released on September 22, 2012. Dumas asked guests about their hardest moments in business, what they learned from them, the idea that became a turning point, and how they actually put it into action. At the end, he included a rapid-fire Q&A with a series of short questions. The format was designed so listeners could follow one successful person each day and see what that person went through and what choices they made.

People did not flock in from the start. In Dumas's recollection, downloads for the first four days were zero. Each morning an episode was released, he sent guests the link and asked them to share it with their readers and followers. As guests began promoting their own interviews, downloads grew, and the show appeared in iTunes' New and Noteworthy featured section.

Having a different entrepreneur on every day also helped with promotion. If today's guest's readers discovered the show, the next day brought a chance to be introduced to another guest's audience. Add in listeners coming from podcast platforms, and within three months of launch, monthly downloads surpassed 100,000.

The booking process also included persistent knocking on doors. Seth Godin rejected Dumas's request to appear four times. When Dumas emailed again on a Thursday, a reply came in about 20 seconds inviting him to do the interview the next day at 1 p.m. Dumas canceled a dentist appointment scheduled for that time and did the interview.

## Batching Recordings and Building Revenue Through Sponsorships

He did not have to record every day to release a show every day. In a 2013 interview, Dumas explained that every Monday he scheduled eight interviews at one-hour intervals and completed editing and uploading in about 16 hours. On Thursdays, he bunched together his own appearances on other podcasts. On the remaining days, he worked on promotion, product development, and community operations.

His first sponsorship offering was a short ad attached to the end of the show. Starting in late November 2012, he sold 30-second ads for $225 to people launching a book or product. Ebook, personal coaching, and affiliate income also began to trickle in, but from October 2012 through March 2013, expenses exceeded total revenue. Even after he started receiving money, he had to keep growing the show and persevere.

In March 2013, after monthly downloads surpassed 200,000, corporate advertisers began reaching out. Companies such as Audible, LegalZoom, Squarespace, and 99designs showed interest in EOFire. Listeners were preparing to launch businesses or running small companies, making them exactly the customers that businesses selling website building, legal, and design services wanted to meet. That April, EOFire's total revenue reached $12,584, with 85% coming from sponsorships.

Dumas's own appearances on other shows also paid off. On the day his interview on Pat Flynn's Smart Passive Income was released in August 2013, EOFire's new daily email subscribers rose to 86 from 29 the previous day. The following month, it recorded total monthly revenue of $51,301.86, including $17,080 in sponsorship revenue. By then, it was also selling community, educational products, and mentoring alongside advertising.

## Building a Team and Selling the Education the Audience Wanted

The business's growth also included Kate Erickson joining. Erickson, who joined the team in April 2013, took charge of running the community and products and organizing recurring tasks. After that, guest onboarding and episode-sharing requests went out on a schedule using prewritten copy, while uploads, scheduled releases, show notes, and transcript writing were handled by a virtual assistant team. While Dumas stood in front of the microphone, several people behind the scenes were preparing the next episode to go out.

Its own products did not sell well from the start. PodPlatform, which Dumas introduced in 2013, was a service that handled the rest of the podcast production work once the customer recorded, but demand fell short of expectations. The problem the operations team identified was that much of the audience still wanted to learn how to start a podcast in the first place. Dumas shut down the done-for-you service and prepared a product to teach people how to create and grow a show themselves.

That product was Podcasters’ Paradise. It would offer video lessons on production, growth, and monetization together with a community where members could ask questions and help one another, plus regular webinars. The experience of seeing members interact in the already-running entrepreneur community Fire Nation Elite helped. The goal was to create a space where people who got stuck while taking lessons alone would have someone to ask.

The two opened a sales page before the product was finished. After the page went live on September 7, 2013, and was promoted by email, social media, and the podcast, 240 interested people gathered within five days. Then, over 72 hours from September 13 to 15, they sold 35 lifetime memberships at $197 each. The education community, which had not yet opened, gained its first paying members, and it officially launched on October 31.

## Expanding Products with a Journal and Paid Interviews

The questions listeners asked most often also led to other products. While interviewing founders, Dumas repeatedly saw the ability to set goals and follow through to the end, and listeners struggled with that execution. So he created The Freedom Journal, a journal designed to help achieve one goal over 100 days. Even the physical product that sat on a desk and was used daily had started from the show.

The advertising business kept growing as well. In a January 2016 interview, Dumas said he was earning $80,000 to $120,000 a month from a 15-second pre-roll before the show and a 60-second mid-roll in the middle. He read the ads himself, coordinating copy with advertisers while keeping the tone he usually used with listeners. The advertising product even included turning the message advertisers wanted into Dumas’s own words.

Paid interviews were added to the monetization model: Dumas said in 2022 Bloomberg Businessweek reporting that he charges guests $3,500 to appear on his show, and he estimated that in June 2022, 20 to 30 percent of sponsorship revenue came from guest appearance fees paid by guests. Founders who wanted to promote their business and products bought the opportunity to speak to the EOFire audience.

## Disclosing the Business’s Numbers for 136 Months

In December 2024, EOFire’s total revenue, including sponsorships, reached $131,587. That same month, the education community Podcasters’ Paradise recorded $4,918, journal sales recorded $2,709, and affiliate income recorded $3,365. A team business centered on founder interviews and selling advertising, education, and physical products together had taken hold.

Dumas and Kate also consistently made their own business process public as content. Starting in September 2013, they recorded in monthly reports how much they earned and spent, what products they were preparing, and what was not working. The practice continued for 136 consecutive months, until the December 2024 report, when they said they would pause disclosures for a while. They answered, month by month with their own business, the same questions they had posed to other founders.
