Jenn Lueke’s Jenn Eats Goood is a content business that has grown by easing people’s meal-planning concerns. Its core product is a paid newsletter that organizes everything from grocery lists to the order of meal preparation. In a July 2026 Creator Spotlight interview, her subscriber base at the time was reported to generate approximately $670,000 in gross annualized subscription revenue.

## The Banker Who Posted Food Photos

Cooking had long been part of Jenn’s daily life. As a child, she learned to cook beside her mother and bought cookbooks with birthday money; in high school, she made dinner for her family herself. In college, she studied finance and entrepreneurship, and in 2018 she began posting photos of the food she made on Instagram.

After graduation, she worked at a bank for about three years, taking food photos and managing a website after work and on weekends. When she left the bank, her Instagram following was still about 5,000. For her next job, Jenn chose creator Liz Moody’s team, and for about a year she experienced another creator’s business up close while also growing her own account.

## Five Dinners Made from $75 of Groceries

The turning point was the grocery series she started in January 2023. She showed the process of buying $75 worth of ingredients and making five different dishes, at least 10 servings of food. She connected different menus—such as chicken soup, curry meatballs, and cauliflower shrimp fried rice—through a single grocery list. Viewers could buy the same ingredients and follow the videos to make five dinners for two people.

This meal plan was designed to use ingredients without waste. Kale was divided between the chicken soup and a chickpea salad, and one can of coconut milk was used half in the soup and half in the curry meatballs. With less need to fill a new shopping cart for every dish, it became possible to enjoy the pleasure of eating a range of dishes and save on food costs at the same time. In an interview at the time, Jenn also explained that she first chooses the dishes she will make for the week and then looks for ingredients that can be used across multiple dishes.

Jenn began production on the next series within a day or two of the first series starting to spread. She then continued a plan that made 10 servings with $50 and then 20 servings, and refined her explanations by reading comments. In 2023, she began devoting herself to her content business, and at the time of the July 2026 interview, her followers combined across platforms exceeded 3,000,000.

## A Paid Subscription That Delivers Meal Plans

Once the grocery series found its footing, a space was needed to deliver meal plans and cooking resources in more detail. In January 2024, Jenn launched the eat goood newsletter on Substack. She gathered recipes and grocery resources that had been included in Instagram post captions and grew it into a space that also guided meal plans and the prep process.

At first, she sent one free newsletter and one paid newsletter each week, and later increased this to two free and one paid per week. At the time of the July 2026 interview, the subscription fee was $8 per month or $60 per year, and she had kept the price set at launch. Jenn continued adding resources included in the paid subscription while providing ample free content.

At the center of the materials paid subscribers received were meal plans that arrived on Fridays. Three times a month, she provided weekly dinner menus with grocery lists attached, and guided subscribers through subscriber-only recipes, parts to prepare in advance, ways to reduce cooking time, and substitute ingredients. All previously published meal plans and recipes remained accessible, so subscribers could follow new plans or return to meal plans they had liked.

## Resources for a Month of Prep and Reader Requests

The plan for preparing a month of breakfasts and lunches broadened the product's scope further. The May 2026 resource divided a month's meals for one person by week and was structured so prep could be completed within two hours each week. If two people are eating, ingredient amounts are doubled, and it also covered grocery lists, cooking order, refrigerator and freezer storage methods, and weekend meals.

Improvements reflecting reader requests were also specific. So that readers would not have to flip back and forth between the recipe and the prep schedule, ingredient amounts and storage methods were included in the schedule, and foods to take out of the freezer that week were also listed. She provided separate PDFs for reading on a phone and for printing and use, and structured nutrition information so only readers who wanted it could open it.

She also promoted the newsletter consistently. She used an automation tool that sent a sign-up link when people commented a specific word on Instagram posts, and after launching the newsletter, she directed people to subscribe in almost every post. The total email subscriber count reported in July 2026 exceeded 180,000, including free subscribers, and the largest source was Instagram.

## Reader Response Leads to Brand Collaborations

In the early days of the business, brand collaborations were the main source of income. Later, newsletter subscriptions grew into the center of the business, while brand collaborations, advertising on her own website, and affiliate-link income continued alongside it. When working with brands, Jenn preferred long-term collaborations that bundled multiple posts, and some sponsored content also ran in the newsletter.

When pitching collaborations to brands, she used data on affiliate-link clicks and orders. For example, after introducing an oven pan, if reader response was strong, she would take that data to the brand and propose a video collaboration. Paying attention to which products drew interest in her regular content led into advertising sales.

## A Newsletter and Cookbook Built with a Team

As production volume grew, she also built a team. At the time of the 2026 interview, two full-time employees handled the newsletter schedule, material production, design, and other tasks, while an external video editor and photographer also contributed to core work. While the employees took care of what was needed for publication, Jenn could spend her time developing and testing new recipes.

The cookbook, which she began preparing in early 2024, involved about two years of work. Jenn tested recipes during the day and wrote the manuscript at night, using a color-coded spreadsheet to track development and testing progress. She also hired professional recipe testers to check whether the recipes worked properly, whether the instructions were easy to understand, and whether they were suitable for following on a weeknight. She also spent considerable time calculating ingredient quantities to fit the meal plans and aligning the shopping lists.

The result, Don’t Think About Dinner, was published on January 13, 2026. It contains more than 125 recipes, 12 weeks of dinner plans, and shopping lists, and it guides readers from how to substitute ingredients to storing and reheating leftovers. True to the book’s title, it is designed to reduce the time readers spend worrying about what to make for dinner each night. After publication, she introduced her cooking and meal planning to more people through an appearance on Good Morning America and a book tour.
