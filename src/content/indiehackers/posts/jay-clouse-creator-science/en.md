Jay Clouse's Creator Science is an education and media business that teaches creators how to make a living from content. It gathers readers through a free newsletter and podcast, sells courses and a paid community, and also earns advertising and affiliate commissions. In an Indie Bites interview released on April 5, 2023, the process by which the business grew to over $40,000 in monthly revenue toward the end of 2022 was described.

## From creator coaching to a full-time business

The starting point was Unreal Collective, a group business coaching program launched in 2017. Jay ran a 12-week program in which he reviewed participants' businesses with them, and he initially expected startup founders to be the main clients. The actual clients were freelancers and creators who were highly skilled at their craft but lacked experience in sales and business operations, and meeting them changed the topics of the education.

This experience led to a collaboration with Smart Passive Income, or SPI, run by Pat Flynn. In 2020, he helped design and launch SPI's paid community, and after SPI acquired Unreal Collective, Jay took charge of the community organization. He continued to run his own newsletter and podcast, and as his personal business grew, he left SPI in early 2022 and returned to working full-time on his own.

What supported his return to full-time work was a change in his revenue structure. In a January 2022 article, Jay explained that his business had 24 detailed income sources, including products and affiliate partners, and that no single one was enough to support him but together they made full-time work possible. While he worked at SPI and reduced services such as consulting, other income grew, and after confirming that change through monthly books, he decided to go independent.

## Building an audience with free content and clarifying the brand

The podcast launched in March 2020 under the name Creative Elements. It was a show that interviewed creators about how they made a living from their work, and before launch he prepared music, visuals, and pilot episodes and collaborated with the podcast network Podglomerate. The network helped with media introductions and placement in podcast apps, and in exchange for taking on ad sales and management, it received a share of revenue. This kind of distribution partner played a role in the show's early growth.

The newsletter was a foundation built over a longer period. It had been published since 2017, but even in August 2020 it had only 1,800 subscribers, and total email subscribers did not cross 10,000 until February 2022. At the time, one major growth path was adding a subscription form to existing posts that drew search visitors, and separate sign-up pages for receiving presentation materials or free resources were also used. For visitors who read a post and left, this provided both a reason to receive the next piece of content and a path to subscribe.

As the business grew, he consolidated a brand that had been scattered under several names. In August 2022, he renamed the newsletter Creator Science, and at the end of March 2023, he unified the podcast under the same name. Jay said he had the problem of people misrepresenting the business or being confused about what he did because of the different names. It was a decision to put the theme of analyzing creators' business growth into the name and to gather attention gained on social media under a single brand.

## Turning Education and Community into Products

The products he sold came from things he had actually done. He turned podcast production and community management into courses, and he earned affiliate commissions by introducing tools he used, such as Circle or, at the time, ConvertKit. From the seven courses he produced with LinkedIn Learning, he received royalties based on usage, and he separately sold consulting and content advertising. By connecting his own education, other companies' tools, and advertisers' products to the same creator customers, he expanded his income streams.

The biggest turning point was The Lab, a paid community launched in March 2022. In a September article that year, Jay said he had reached 135 members and $96,000 in cumulative sales about six months after launch. It was the moment when years of creator-focused content and community management experience combined into a single paid product.

What The Lab provided was an environment where creators could keep asking questions and receiving feedback as they ran their businesses. Jay had 30-minute one-on-one calls with new members, joined live sessions, and personally answered members' posts. Each month he also released a retrospective video explaining his profit and loss statement, experiment results, and decisions for the next month. Members could look into the operator's actual business and solve problems in their own businesses together.

At launch, the base prices were $999 per year for Standard and $1,999 per year for VIP, with a 50% discount for early participants. In early September 2022, he raised them to $1,499 and $2,499 per year, respectively. The approach was to offer a lower price to customers who joined early and then raise the price for new sign-ups as membership and operating experience accumulated.

At the same time, Jay set a membership cap at 200. There were two goals: helping members get to know one another and have in-depth conversations, and managing the time he could spend with each member. The judgment was that if running the community took all his time, he would not be able to create the newsletter and podcast, and the new practical experience he could deliver to members would also decrease.

## Reading Monthly Revenue, Prepaid Payments, and Operating Costs Together

The price increase and membership cap moved readers who had been delaying joining. In September 2022, total business revenue was about $57,663, of which more than $41,000 came from The Lab. At the time, membership accepted only annual payments, so this monthly revenue included a full year of dues prepaid by new members. To understand the monthly figure, therefore, one has to look at both when new members joined and when they renewed the following year.

December 2022 revenue in the public books was $40,340. The approximate breakdown Jay described was $18,000 from membership, $13,000 from sponsorship, $3,100 from digital products, $2,100 from affiliate commissions, and $2,200 each from royalties and services. Because sponsorship payments for several months were sometimes deposited at once, that month's revenue also reflected the timing gap between when ads ran and when they were settled.

Annual revenue in 2022 was about $337,000, and average monthly revenue over the last four months exceeded $40,000. The same December books recorded about $14,000 in expenses and about $26,200 in income after deductions, from which owner compensation and taxes were then paid. He also explained that product revenue in those books was based on deposited amounts after payment-processing fees were deducted. At the time, about $7,600 of the expenses went to contract labor helping with audio and video production. It was an operating structure in which Jay was at the center of the business and content, while some production work was handled by collaborators.

## Changing the Membership Cap and Expanding into Digital Products

He also strengthened the connections among products. In a 2023 interview, Jay explained that he folded courses and resources previously sold separately into The Lab, and that customers who had first bought a course could receive credit for their prior payment and move into membership by paying only the difference. Customers could use a smaller product to check the quality of the instruction and then buy deeper support, and The Lab reached its 200-member cap in February 2023.

After the cap filled, a new problem arose. In an October 2023 interview, Jay acknowledged that because his best-selling product had a signup limit, the path from newsletter reader to repeat purchases was blocked. A recommendation quiz that asked visitors questions and suggested suitable products sold about three per day, but the lack of a next purchase step meant that acquiring readers through paid advertising still did not break even. The cap that had protected the member experience began to limit the business's further growth.

In January 2024, Jay got members' agreement to remove the 200-person cap and switch to an application model. The rationale was that having more members who lived in the same area or had experience with a particular platform would enrich offline meetups and knowledge exchange. The perception around the cap lingered, however, creating a problem in which people thought it was already full even though they could actually join. The scarcity message that had once encouraged signups blocked new applications after the policy changed.

That year, he increased digital product sales to reduce dependence on the community. The flagship product, CreatorHQ, packaged the Notion workspace he had built to run Creator Science so other creators could use it, and its four-day launch in April 2024 generated more than $60,000 in sales. A January 2025 retrospective reported $131,856 in cumulative sales and 733 users since launch. By expanding even the tools he built to run his business into sellable products, he created a way to grow revenue beyond continually adding members.
