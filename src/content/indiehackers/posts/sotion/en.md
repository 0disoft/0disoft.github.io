## Buying a Business That Already Has Customers

Bruce McLachlan started out as a programmer, moved through software design and engineering management, and became the head of technology at the fintech company Backbase. He kept his own projects going alongside his job, and in 2021 and 2022 he set out to turn them into a second source of income.

One of his early attempts was Namejax, a trading service for people who wanted to secure a domain and matching social media handles at the same time. He sold roughly 20 domains he had acquired himself and made $975 in total, but he never grew it into a marketplace where buyers and sellers gathered. The product was needed at the exact moment someone created a brand, which made it hard to reach customers at the right time, and it took more operational work than the competition. Bruce shut the business down in May 2022 and shared the outcome.

While running that experiment he also looked for small software businesses to acquire. After about ten months of searching and reviewing, on February 16, 2022, he bought Cloakist from its existing operator, Louis Barclay, for $60,000. At the time it had about 160 customers and roughly $2,000 in monthly recurring revenue. The purchase price came to about 2.5 times annual recurring revenue.

What Bruce looked for was paying customers, stable technology, a working acquisition channel, and room to improve. Cloakist kept bringing in new signups, but there was space to improve paid conversion, and the website and customer guidance still needed work. He planned to build on the existing technology while tightening the signup flow, follow-up emails, and search traffic.

Cloakist is a service that puts a custom domain and branding on public pages built with tools like Notion, Airtable, and Calendly. Users connect the address of the original page, then set their own domain, logo, and colors. They keep managing the content in the tool they already know while showing visitors their own website address. The setup saves them from rebuilding a booking page or a resource list as a separate website.

## Growing Revenue Through Signup and Search

After the acquisition, he started with the signup flow for new customers. He removed the requirement to enter a card when starting a free trial, and changed the trial period to start counting when the customer's site actually went live. That prevented trial time from draining before setup was finished and let customers see a working result first.

In June 2022 he reported that monthly recurring revenue had reached about $2,500. The same update listed 161 new signups and 13 paid conversions. That put conversion at about 8%, and alongside the revenue growth Bruce kept watching paid conversion and churn.

Support expanded to Trello, Loom, Microsoft Sway, and others. At the time, most new customers arrived through organic search, followed by the user forums of each of those products. Adding more integrations and reaching the people already using those products moved forward together.

He cut down on repetitive work when producing content for search. In September 2022 he moved 25 existing posts from Ghost to a setup where Notion handled management and Feather handled publishing. Shared passages were maintained as Notion synced blocks, and templates plus property values produced 15 new pages. It was a system where one edit could update the same text across many posts.

The topics themselves were matched to tasks customers would search for. In 2023 the Cloakist blog covered how to connect a custom domain to a Linktree page, how to serve a Canva page from your own address, and how to connect a domain to Typeform. The content explained the problem people hit at the next step after choosing a tool, and introduced Cloakist as part of the solution.

## Going Deeper into Notion Membership Management

Sotion began as a Notion page password protection tool that Cloakist's previous operator released in February 2021. Bruce took it over and expanded it, adding email signups for visitors in 2022. Managing who could see content written in Notion started to become the center of the product.

In December 2022 he showed how to connect Notion and Gumroad to build a paid membership site. Notion handled writing and organizing content, Gumroad handled payments, and Sotion handled linking buyers to access. Content sellers could use courses or ebooks they had already written while connecting payments to a members-only page. It reduced the burden of building a membership site from scratch.

The content customers distributed included online courses, paid reports, ebooks, and resource libraries. They needed a way to deliver finished material to specific people and control who could see it. In March 2023 Bruce named password protection as a particularly popular Sotion feature. Without offering a full set of complex site-building tools, controlling the conditions for access to material alone was enough to meet a concrete demand.

In 2024 he added the connections needed to run paid memberships. March brought paid membership through Stripe, and April through Lemon Squeezy. In May he improved the email login flow so the logo, reply address, and other details could be configured. The range of work content sellers could run under their own brand stretched from payment through login instructions.

Sotion's revenue model is a monthly or annual subscription, with plans split by number of sites, number of members, and feature range. Customers manage content in Notion and subscribe to the features needed for distribution and membership operations. The same product covers everything from a small membership site to managing multiple sites and many members.

Then on June 25, 2024, Notion announced Notion Sites. It brought custom domain connections, page styling, search metadata settings, and Google Analytics integration inside Notion. For customers who only needed basic publishing and a domain connection, an option appeared that required no outside service.

Two days later, on June 27, Bruce published a post that led with Sotion's membership features. He presented courses, content hubs, and customer resource libraries built on passwords, email signup, allowed email lists, and paid membership features. The access control features he had already built became the reason Sotion stood apart from Notion's built-in site features. He pitched the product to customers who needed to deliver content to specific people and keep managing it, rather than to those who just wanted a public page.

## The Weight of Operations and Growth of Two Services

As the business grew, AWS costs spiked and he had to redesign the core infrastructure. Changes to outside platforms broke customer sites, and malicious users built phishing and scam pages. Bruce tightened operations by building incident response and abuse monitoring.

Running a job and a business in parallel for about three years took a toll. Even after moving to a role with less pressure he dealt with burnout, and in July 2025 he became a full-time indie hacker. Revenue at that point was not enough to cover his living costs entirely, but he chose independence based on the money he had set aside.

In October 2025 he reported combined monthly recurring revenue of more than $12,000 across Cloakist and Sotion. By then Sotion, which focused on Notion, had overtaken Cloakist, which supported several platforms.

Sotion v2, announced in April 2026, divided access control more finely. Where the previous version applied a single access condition to an entire site, the new version let you set visibility and access conditions per page and use member groups. The direction was to keep public material and members-only material on one site while giving each person a different reading range.

It also included a feature that links items in a Notion database to customers and shows each customer only what they need. Agencies can use it to deliver work per client, and consultants to deliver material per engagement. What Sotion handles grew from putting passwords on pages into delivering content per customer and running access control.
