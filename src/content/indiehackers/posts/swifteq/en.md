## Finding a Product in Zendesk's Repetitive Work

In 2020, the work Sorin Alupoaie chose when he started Swifteq was merging duplicate customer inquiries. In Zendesk, the customer support software, each inquiry is managed as a ticket, and when the same customer gets in touch several times, an agent has to find the related tickets and tidy them up. Agents also ended up answering the same problem separately. Sorin's first product was an app that automated this repetitive work: Merge Duplicate Tickets.

He had been through products before that failed to grow properly. He had also run a business on outside investment and felt his freedom to make decisions shrink as the tension grew. With Swifteq he wanted to take responsibility for the product and the customers himself and build a profitable business that could support his family. Starting alone and taking no outside money were choices aimed at that goal.

He began by narrowing the customer down to support teams using Zendesk. Reading Zendesk's official community and posts from Support Driven, where support practitioners gather, he looked for the complaints that kept coming up. The problem of merging duplicate tickets by hand stood out, and he judged that existing solutions left room for improvement in both features and price. The people who would use the product and the problem they had set its starting point.

The early product included only the features it needed. According to the development approach Sorin described in 2023, most apps offered only enough of a screen for users to switch them on and off, and he handled the complicated configuration on his own server side. Apart from two exceptions, he never spent more than a week on the first version of an app. He allocated development time by watching how customers actually used the product and then filling in features and settings.

## Adding Automation to the Existing Support Flow

The ticket merging app connected into how Zendesk already handled work. When a new ticket appears, Zendesk's triggers and webhooks pass it to the app, and the app checks the configured criteria to decide whether it is a duplicate and merges the tickets. It could match on shared identifiers such as an order number as well as on the same customer, and it could leave out tickets created by internal staff or bots. Support teams kept the system they already used and added automation that followed their own working rules.

Sorin found similar demand in the work of managing help center articles. Help Center Manager bundled features to replace terms across many articles at once, find and fix broken links, and duplicate or export articles. With a tool like this, the person running the help center could avoid fixing articles one by one or asking for a separate script. The work actually needed to manage the content piling up in Zendesk became the product's scope.

The outdoor route service Komoot is a case that shows this demand. According to a customer interview Swifteq published, Komoot had built its own collection tooling and reports to see how well its help center performed, but the results were incomplete and took time to maintain. Running documentation in six languages also meant moving translations and rebuilding HTML formatting. After adopting Swifteq, the team could use the analysis reports it needed and translate while keeping the original formatting, and the person in charge said the time spent producing and managing translations dropped by half.

The buying process at the home exchange service HomeExchange was more direct. At the time described in a public case from the company, its inquiry volume averaged 23,000 a month, and members who ran into trouble while travelling contacted support several times in a short span, which pushed up duplicate tickets. The person leading the support project found Swifteq in the Zendesk Marketplace, calculated the expected time savings, and proposed a free trial. After a few rounds of adjusting the setup they confirmed the effect, and the automatic merge volume cited in the case passed 10,000 a month.

The relationship with HomeExchange also led to a new feature. When a customer sent a thank-you note on a solved ticket, the ticket reopened and the agent had to handle it a second time. The HomeExchange contact asked Swifteq about the problem, and the team used its existing automation app to send these tickets back to solved. The speed of responding to a small annoyance discovered in the field and the quality of the support built trust for the next purchase.

## Selling by Usage and by the Nature of the Work

The pricing also reflected the nature of the work. The ticket merging app splits its monthly price tiers by how many tickets it checks for duplicates each month, and even the entry tier includes custom rules and exclusion conditions. Cleaning up a backlog of past tickets all at once is sold as a separate service. Ongoing inquiries became a subscription and one-off cleanup a separate charge, turning two different kinds of demand into revenue.

The way he met customers was matched to the same group of practitioners. Sorin publicized Swifteq by offering free tools and useful articles, with help from Supported Content, a content studio in the support field, and specialist writers. The topics came from the questions and discussions that moved through Support Driven. The customer observation he used to build products also fed the content customers would search for and read.

The results Sorin published in January 2023 were 120 paying customers and 5,000 euros in monthly recurring revenue. The customer list included companies such as FREE NOW, Zapier, and Hotjar. In June of that year annual recurring revenue reached 100,000 euros. A set of apps solving a few specific jobs was starting to become a recurring line item for business customers.

In December 2023 he recorded 230 customers and 150,000 euros in annual recurring revenue. By this point Sorin said he could pay himself a proper salary. He could run the business without relying on another job or his savings, and decide for himself which products to build and where to spend his time. Swifteq's revenue supported the independence he had wanted when he founded it.

## Operating Challenges That Emerged with Growth

As customers grew, so did the operating burden. In the second half of 2023 churn increased, growth in the main apps stalled, and support requests ate into the time he had for product development. Sorin went on to automate part of the trial and customer management process and, with the help of professional writers, rebuilt the help documentation. He kept filling in the documentation to match customer questions and cut down the time spent repeating explanations.

Pricing was one of the judgments Sorin corrected late. In a 2024 retrospective he admitted that he had sold most of his apps too cheaply and had taken too long to fix the prices. Low prices delayed the point at which he could secure profitability and reinvest in the business, by his account. Help Center Manager and the analytics tools in particular had more value for mid-sized and large companies, so the price needed to reflect that value and the profit required to keep the business running.

Being a founder who was comfortable building products created another blind spot. Sorin said he missed a search indexing problem on his website and took a hit to search traffic for several months, and that by keeping too much of the technical work to himself he lost time he could have spent growing the business. He concluded he should have hired developers earlier and put resources into marketing and sales. Once a product starts selling, leaving time for customer acquisition and operations becomes important.

Relationships formed inside the Zendesk ecosystem also brought customers. Independent consultants and partners who set up Zendesk for companies recommended Swifteq when they ran into needs that basic features could not solve. Relationships with Zendesk employees who work with customers helped too. The people who knew what was needed in the field became the product's advocates.

In June 2024 he passed 300 customers and reached 20,000 euros in monthly recurring revenue. At that point Sorin said the business he had founded alone, with no outside investment, was profitable. In an interview published in November 2025 the monthly recurring revenue he reported was about 44,000 euros. Growth varied from month to month, but a business selling practical apps on repeat kept getting bigger.

## Choosing Customer Problems amid Platform Changes

Alongside the growth, the risk of depending on a platform became real. In a September 2025 post Sorin explained that Zendesk had shipped its own help center translation feature that overlapped directly with a Swifteq product, but two months after the launch revenue was still rising and customers had stayed. In a November interview the same year he said a Zendesk attachment cleanup feature had increased churn in a related app and that he expected a loss of revenue as well. How a platform's expansion of its own features affected the business varied by product, and Sorin could not control those decisions.

To reduce the dependency he moved his help center translation app to Intercom. The two platforms' APIs differ enough that he had to redesign parts of the structure, so it was not a matter of copying the existing product across. He also found that demand was small when he avoided the possibility of overlapping with Zendesk by focusing on peripheral features such as generating image alt text. Afterwards he shifted his criteria for choosing products toward solving the problems customers actually needed, even at the risk of competing with the platform.

The reason to buy that runs through Swifteq's story was the time it took to finish work in the field. Komoot was spending time on its own reports and translation management, HomeExchange was spending time cleaning up duplicate inquiries, and Swifteq offered that work as a product several companies could use. The value of the subscription came from reducing the repetitive work of support teams together with the development, configuration, and maintenance that supports it. Sorin built his business around helping companies get more out of software they were already using.
