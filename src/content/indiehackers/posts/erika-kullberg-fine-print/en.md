In Erika Kullberg's videos, a customer and an employee argue over a refund or compensation. Wearing different wigs, Kullberg moves between the two roles, and when the customer side points out the company's terms, the situation flips. At the end of the video, an explanation follows that a lawyer named Erika read the terms and informed them. By turning long documents people skim over into short stories about protecting their own money, she made her name known to the public.

At the time of a June 2024 Entrepreneur interview, Kullberg's YouTube subscribers exceeded 2,000,000, and combined followers across multiple social media platforms totaled more than 21,000,000. In the same period, the YouTube income she disclosed was $353,135.99. This amount was the cumulative pretax total paid by YouTube over about five years since she started the channel in 2019, and brand sponsorship revenue was separate.

## A Corporate Lawyer Repaying Student Debt

The reason she first threw herself into studying money was her own student loans. After graduating from Georgetown Law, the debt she faced was over $200,000. Even while earning a high income as a corporate lawyer, she kept her living costs at student levels and set a goal to repay $9,000 per month. After changing the loan terms and managing expenses, she repaid over $225,000 in total over about two years.

The annual salary of the corporate law job Kullberg left was $250,000. She said she became a lawyer because she wanted to help people but wanted to escape a life of handling large corporate transactions and living according to her boss's schedule. In 2019 she left the law firm and founded Plug and Law, a legal service for founders and small business owners, and late that year started a YouTube channel covering personal finance.

## The Promise to Post 52 Videos in a Year

The commitment she made when starting YouTube was to post one video every week for a year, 52 videos in total. She set a publishing schedule she could stick to, so that a lukewarm response to the first few videos would not make her quit. In the beginning, she kept this commitment by spending 15 to 20 hours per video.

Before filming, she also spent time studying other channels. She found creators covering the same topics, sorted their videos by popularity, and examined which subjects drew people's attention. She also analyzed titles and thumbnails together, preparing even the process by which people discover and click on videos.

During the first three months, she gathered about 1,000 subscribers, and only after about another two and a half months did she reach 2,000. As several videos spread widely, subscribers grew to more than 50,000 over the next 30 days. At the end of April 2020, she met the watch hours needed for advertising monetization, and on the first day she could run ads, she generated more than $700 in advertising revenue.

## Affiliate and Sponsorship Income Built Alongside Advertising

In May 2020, she earned about $20,000 that month from YouTube ads, affiliate commissions, and sponsorships combined. Before the channel's first anniversary, the cumulative amount from these three income sources exceeded $100,000. In an interview at the time, she explained that most of this income had been generated during the previous five months.

She also set up multiple income streams early on. In addition to ads attached to videos, she received commissions through affiliate links for products or services she recommended and took on sponsorships in which she introduced companies' products in videos. At the time, affiliate and sponsorship revenue accounted for a larger share than advertising, and Kullberg said she actively sought out related opportunities.

## Short Skits Showing Consumer Rights

When she expanded into TikTok, a $1,000 bet with the condition that she post videos every day drove her to follow through. Later, a roughly 30-second video addressing a Nike shoe refund and warranty issue spread widely. In a later Forbes interview, she described how that video gained her 1,000,000 followers in 24 hours.

The topics covered in the short skits were everyday problems such as sneaker product warranties, compensation for being denied boarding on a flight, and coffee refills. She used dialogue to show what conditions consumers should check and how they should speak to employees. The lines, which made polite requests while precisely pointing to the necessary grounds, were easy for viewers to apply to their own situations.

## Different Platform Payouts for the Same Videos

The earnings from longer videos stand out in her per-video payout breakdown. In material released in 2024, a roughly 12-minute video explaining why she quit her job recorded about 3,800,000 views and cumulative earnings of $45,639.14. A 48-second Short about negotiating medical bills had about 4,100,000 views and cumulative earnings of $106.85. Erika Kullberg disclosed individual videos' length, views, and payout together in this way.

There were also large differences in platform-by-platform payouts. In the same public video, Kullberg explained that the money she received for posting on TikTok for about two years totaled $5,756, while on Instagram, even with about 5,300,000 followers, platform payments for videos were $0. She posted the videos completed for TikTok to Facebook as they were and received a total of $20,251 over about two years. By distributing a video she had produced once across multiple places, she secured different income opportunities on each platform.

## A Wider Scope Through a Production Team and a Podcast

Early in the production process, she got help from an editor. When making the first video took 20 hours to learn editing and another 10 hours for the actual editing, she hired an editor. After income began on YouTube, she expanded her collaborations to include a thumbnail designer and others, and in a 2024 interview she explained that multiple people were helping with production.

There was also a time she turned down a large sponsorship fee. In a 2026 Forbes interview, Kullberg disclosed that she had rejected a $100,000 brand deal early in her work, citing the preservation of viewer trust as the reason.

In fall 2022, she launched the podcast Erika Taught Me, broadening the range of stories she could cover. She asked Chris Voss, a former FBI hostage negotiator, about negotiation, and entrepreneur Barbara Corcoran about business experience. By adding other experts' experiences to a personal finance channel where she had previously done the explaining herself, she expanded the topics from consumer rights and money management to negotiation and entrepreneurship.

The response Kullberg especially welcomes is when viewers say they have tried using what they learned in the videos in real life. She said that while paying off student debt, she realized how little she had learned about money in school, and she promised herself that once she cleared the debt, she would teach others how to manage money. In a 2023 Inside Edition interview, she explained that as long as people face money problems, there will continue to be topics she can help with.
